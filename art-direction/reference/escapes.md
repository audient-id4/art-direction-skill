# Escapes

Naming a pattern to avoid is only half a rule — the other half is what goes
there instead. A ban with no replacement gets ignored the moment there is a
deadline.

Each pattern below is common because it *works*. The problem is not that it
is wrong; it is that it arrives before anyone considers alternatives. Use one
if the document argues for it. Do not use one by default.

---

**Hero, subtitle, CTA.**
The pattern assumes the most valuable thing on the page is a sentence about
the product. Usually the most valuable thing is the product. Put the actual
artifact — the interface, the data, the work, the availability — in the first
screen and let it argue. A masthead beside a live thing beats a headline
above a screenshot.

**Three equal cards.**
Three things are rarely equally important, and equal cards say they are. Give
the strongest one more room and let the others sit as a list. Or drop the
containers and use a typographic hierarchy — the card was only ever a fence
around content that could have stood on its own.

**Bento grid.**
Fine when the content genuinely has different weights and shapes. A bento
where every cell holds a heading and a line of text is a three-card layout
with more borders. If the cells are interchangeable, it is decoration.

**Everything in cards.**
A card says "discrete unit, separate from its neighbours". Most page content
is not that. Alternatives: full-bleed sections divided by rules, content
directly on the ground, overlapping panels, an editorial two-column with a
wide margin for asides.

**One radius everywhere.**
A chip and a full-width panel curved identically will look wrong at one of
them. Three to five values by role. And nested radii must be concentric —
`inner = outer − padding` — or the corner gap pinches.

**Violet-to-blue gradient.**
It marks a page as generated more reliably than any other single element. If
depth is the goal, get it from layered near-neutrals at different
temperatures. If emphasis is the goal, one saturated hue used once does more.
If the flat colour looked plain, the problem was the composition.

**Glass everywhere.**
Backdrop blur is a material for surfaces that float above content. Applied to
static sections it just makes text harder to read and costs a repaint. Use it
where something is genuinely on top of something else.

**Fade-up on scroll, applied to everything.**
Reads as a template with a plugin. Animate what changes state. Content that
was always there does not need to arrive. If a scroll effect earns its place,
give it to exactly one element and make it structural — a pinned column, a
clipped reveal that shows something.

**Icon beside every label.**
An icon that repeats the adjacent word costs comprehension. Icons work where
they replace text, mark status, or aid scanning in a dense list.

**Emoji as texture.**
The single most reliable AI tell. If emoji belong, use system rendering only
and consistently. Otherwise none.

**Pill buttons everywhere.**
Full-round is one voice. Against sharp panels it reads as borrowed from a
different design. Match the button's geometry to the system, and consider
making it the one soft element rather than one of many.

**Perfect symmetry.**
Centred everything is stable and inert. Asymmetry creates direction and lets
one element dominate. If a layout is centred, it should be because stillness
is the point.

**Decorative blobs and abstract 3D.**
Shapes with no referent. If the space needs weight, use the real subject
matter, type at scale, or nothing. Empty space is a legitimate answer and a
rarer one.

**Uniform section padding down the page.**
Even rhythm is no rhythm. Vary it: compress where content is dense, open
before something important. The pause is what makes the next thing land.

**Stock photography.**
People who are not your users, in an office that is not yours. One real
artifact — a screenshot, a document, an object, the actual output —
outperforms any number of them.

**Sub-4.5:1 grey on grey.**
Low contrast reads as "designed" for about a week and as unreadable
permanently. It is also itself a cliché now. Measure.
